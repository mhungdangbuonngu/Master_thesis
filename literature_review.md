## Abstract

Precision oncology depends on the ability to predict how a tumor will respond to a drug, or to a combination of drugs, before treatment begins, yet the deep learning models that now dominate this space remain largely opaque, which limits their clinical trust and their utility for mechanistic hypothesis generation. This review synthesizes the literature on computational drug response prediction (DRP) and drug synergy prediction (DSP/DDS), tracing the field's progression from statistical and generative models through graph neural network (GNN) architectures to the emerging integration of explainable AI (XAI) with GNNs for mechanism-level drug discovery. Thirty-six sources were selected through a four-layer search strategy (Boolean database search, citation chaining, forward citation tracking, and semantic search) combining a user-supplied seed corpus with systematically retrieved supplementary literature, screened against relevance, recency, and methodological rigor criteria appropriate to a fast-moving computer-science/machine-learning domain. The review finds that GNN-based methods have become the dominant architecture for both DRP and DSP because they naturally encode molecular and multi-omic relational structure, that XAI techniques adapted from computer vision and NLP (attention, gradient-based attribution, and perturbation-based subgraph explainers) have been retrofitted onto these GNNs with growing but still uneven rigor, and that only a small number of studies — most notably XGDP (Wang et al., 2025) — combine mechanistic explainability with drug discovery-oriented case validation. Persistent gaps include the near-absence of standardized explanation-quality benchmarks in the DRP/DSP domain, weak external and cross-dataset generalization, and a scarcity of wet-lab-validated interpretability claims. The review proposes a research agenda centered on benchmarked, biologically grounded XAI-GNN pipelines for drug mechanism prediction.

**Keywords**: drug response prediction, drug synergy prediction, graph neural networks, explainable artificial intelligence, GNNExplainer, precision oncology, drug discovery

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Topic and Rationale

Predicting how a cancer cell line, patient-derived model, or tumor will respond to a drug — and how two or more drugs will interact when combined — is one of the central computational problems in precision oncology. Two closely related but distinct prediction tasks structure this literature. Drug response prediction (DRP) asks how a single compound will affect a given biological sample, typically framed as regression over a sensitivity score (e.g., IC50, AUC) or classification into responder/non-responder categories, using cell line multi-omic profiles (gene expression, mutation, copy number) and drug molecular structure as inputs (Okyere et al., 2025; Xiao et al., 2025). Drug synergy prediction (DSP), sometimes called drug-drug synergy (DDS) prediction, extends this to combinatorial regimens, asking whether the joint effect of two drugs exceeds what would be expected from their individual effects — a synergy signal that is central to combination therapy design in oncology (Pan et al., 2023; Abbasi & Rousu, 2024).

Both tasks are underwritten by a small number of large public pharmacogenomic resources — most prominently the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) and Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) panels for single-drug DRP, and combination-screening resources such as NCI-ALMANAC, DrugComb, and the O'Neil dataset for DSP — that recur as training and evaluation substrates across nearly every method reviewed in Sections 2 through 4 (Wang et al., 2025; Peng et al., 2022; Pan et al., 2023). This shared data infrastructure is a double-edged methodological fact: it enables direct comparability between architecturally very different methods, but it also means that many benchmark improvements reported across this literature are measured on overlapping, relatively small, and cell-line-biased screening panels, a limitation that becomes directly relevant when the review turns to cross-dataset generalization in Sections 2.3 and 6.1.

Over the past decade, both tasks have been reshaped by deep learning, and within deep learning, by graph neural networks (GNNs) specifically. Molecules are naturally graphs — atoms as nodes, bonds as edges — and cell lines can be represented as gene-gene interaction, pathway, or similarity networks, making GNNs a structurally appropriate inductive bias for both drug-side and biology-side representation learning (Liu et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2025). This structural fit, combined with GNNs' demonstrated predictive gains over feature-concatenation baselines, has made graph-based architectures the de facto standard in recent DRP/DSP methods (Berry & Cheng, 2025; Besharatifard & Vafaee, 2024).

Yet predictive accuracy alone is insufficient for clinical and drug-discovery adoption. A model that predicts sensitivity without indicating which genes, pathways, or molecular substructures drove that prediction offers little support for mechanism-of-action hypothesis generation, biomarker discovery, or clinician trust — the classical "black box" problem of deep learning applied to biomedicine (Lavecchia, 2025; Gangwal & Lavecchia, 2026). This has motivated a growing body of work applying explainable AI (XAI) techniques — attention mechanisms, gradient-based attribution methods such as Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al., 2017), and perturbation-based subgraph explainers such as GNNExplainer (Ying et al., 2019) — directly to GNN-based DRP/DSP models. The paper that anchors this review's central interest, Wang, Kumar, and Rajapakse's (2025) "Drug discovery and mechanism prediction with explainable graph neural networks" (hereafter XGDP), exemplifies this convergence: it combines GAT/GCN/RGCN/RGAT architectures with GNNExplainer and Integrated Gradients to not only predict drug response but recover biologically interpretable mechanistic evidence (e.g., an EGFR-linked explanation for afatinib sensitivity), illustrating both the promise and the current limits of XAI-GNN integration in this domain. This review is timely because the XAI-GNN subfield for DRP/DSP is still small, fragmented across separate DRP and DSP literatures, and lacks a synthesis that positions it relative to the broader GNN-explainability literature developed primarily in general graph machine learning and molecular property prediction (Kakkad et al., 2023; Rao et al., 2022; Nandan et al., 2025).

### 1.2 Scope and Boundaries

This review covers English-language, primarily computer-science/machine-learning-oriented literature on computational methods for (a) single-drug response prediction in cancer cell lines and related preclinical models, (b) drug synergy/combination prediction, and (c) explainability methods applied to graph neural networks, with particular depth on their intersection in drug discovery and mechanism-of-action prediction. Within this scope, "graph neural network" is used broadly to include convolutional (GCN), attentional (GAT), relational/heterogeneous (RGCN, RGAT), and contrastive-learning-augmented variants, since the reviewed literature itself does not treat these as separate research programs but as points along a shared design space that individual DRP/DSP papers combine and extend; the review does not, however, extend its architectural scope to graph transformers or diffusion-based graph generative models, which appear in the adjacent molecular-generation literature but not in the DRP/DSP-specific corpus surveyed here. The review privileges methods published between 2019 and 2026, reflecting the period in which deep generative and graph-based methods became dominant, while including foundational architectural and XAI papers published earlier (e.g., Kipf & Welling, 2017; Veličković et al., 2018) because they are load-bearing prerequisites for nearly all downstream GNN-based DRP/DSP work. Consistent with a computer-science/machine-learning evidence profile, the review admits high-quality arXiv and bioRxiv preprints and peer-reviewed conference proceedings (e.g., NeurIPS, ICLR, ICML) alongside journal articles, reflecting this field's publication norms, in which foundational architectural contributions (GCN, GAT, GNNExplainer) are canonically cited as conference papers rather than journal articles. The review excludes methods focused exclusively on drug-target interaction prediction, drug-drug pharmacokinetic interaction (adverse event) prediction, de novo molecular generation, and non-graph-based DRP/DSP methods except where needed as historical or comparative context (e.g., variational autoencoder methods that predate the graph-based turn). Clinical-trial-level pharmacogenomic studies and wet-lab pharmacology papers without a computational prediction component are also out of scope.

### 1.3 Review Methodology

Sources were identified through a four-layer progressive search strategy. First, a curated seed set of 18 candidate sources was supplied by the requester, spanning seven review/survey articles, ten specific DRP/DSP methods, and one software library; each was independently verified for bibliographic accuracy via targeted web search rather than accepted at face value. One title in the seed set ("A review of machine learning approaches for drug synergy prediction in cancer," attributed to 2025) was found to correspond to two distinct real papers with near-identical titles: Torkamannia, Omidi, and Ferdousi's (2022) *Briefings in Bioinformatics* review, which matches the "machine learning" wording exactly but not the year, and Li, Zhang, Zheng, and Su's (2025) *npj Drug Discovery* review, which matches the year but is titled "...deep learning approaches..." rather than "...machine learning approaches...". Both are included in this review and are cited explicitly here to flag the discrepancy for the reader, since silently selecting one risked either an anachronistic citation or an inexact title match. Second, citation chaining was applied to the seed papers' reference lists to surface foundational and closely related work (e.g., the GCN, GAT, and Integrated Gradients papers underlying nearly all reviewed GNN and XAI methods). Third, forward citation tracking and semantic search (via web search over Google Scholar-indexed and publisher databases) were used to identify papers explicitly at the intersection of GNNs and explainability for drug response/synergy prediction, since this intersection was underrepresented in the seed set relative to the requester's stated primary interest. This yielded fourteen supplementary sources, including XAI-GNN methods (XMR, DRExplainer, SANEpool, AttenSyn), XAI-GNN surveys (Kakkad et al., 2023; Nandan et al., 2025; Rao et al., 2022; Gangwal & Lavecchia, 2026; Berry & Cheng, 2025; Zhang et al., 2025), and foundational GNN/XAI method papers. Fourth, each candidate source was screened using a peer-review/preprint-quality check, a recency check (favoring 2019–2026 unless foundational), a research-question relevance check, and a methodological soundness check, consistent with a computer-science/machine-learning domain evidence profile that admits arXiv preprints and top-tier conference proceedings on equal footing with journal articles. In total, 36 sources were retained: 18 from the original seed list (with the R4 duplicate resolved by inclusion of both variants) and 18 supplementary sources (14 XAI-GNN-focused plus 4 additional confirmatory/foundational sources located during verification). This falls within the review-type source count range (30–80 sources) appropriate for a thesis-chapter-length literature review.

### 1.4 Organization of the Review

Section 2 reviews the foundations of computational DRP and DSP, covering the statistical-to-generative-deep-learning methods that preceded and coexist with the graph-based turn. Section 3 reviews GNN architectures specifically, for both single-drug response prediction and combination synergy prediction, establishing the architectural vocabulary (GCN, GAT, relational and heterogeneous variants, contrastive learning) that XAI methods are subsequently applied to. Section 4 — the review's thematic center of gravity, given the requester's stated primary interest — reviews explainable AI methods for GNNs in general and their specific applications to drug response, synergy, and mechanism prediction, with XGDP (Wang et al., 2025) treated in depth as the paper of central interest. Section 5 synthesizes convergent findings, unresolved debates, and methodological patterns across all three themes. Section 6 identifies concrete gaps and proposes a research agenda. Section 7 concludes with practical implications for researchers designing the next generation of interpretable DRP/DSP systems.

## 2. Foundations of Computational Drug Response and Drug Synergy Prediction

### 2.1 Single-Drug Response Prediction: From Statistical Models to Generative Deep Learning

Before graph-based architectures became dominant, DRP methods largely treated cell lines and drugs as fixed-length feature vectors — gene expression profiles, mutation calls, or molecular fingerprints — fed into regression, kernel, or early feed-forward neural network models. Okyere et al. (2025) provide the most recent systematic account of this landscape, proposing a unified classification framework organized around the structural relationship between input data and response variable, and noting that despite two decades of methodological churn, no single input representation or model family dominates across all evaluation settings — a theme that recurs throughout this review. Their framework further distinguishes between models trained to predict a continuous sensitivity score directly from static baseline features, and models — the generative family discussed below — that instead learn an intermediate latent representation intended to denoise or disentangle biological signal from technical assay noise before prediction, a distinction that maps closely onto why generative modeling emerged as a distinct methodological thread rather than a mere architectural variant of regression. Xiao et al. (2025) reinforce this point empirically, conducting a comprehensive investigation of drug molecular representations (fingerprints, descriptors, and learned graph embeddings) across multiple machine learning backbones and finding that representation choice interacts strongly with model architecture and dataset, such that no representation is universally superior — an early signal that the field's rapid adoption of graph representations was an empirical bet rather than a settled conclusion. Notably, Xiao et al.'s (2025) comparison already includes learned graph-based molecular embeddings alongside classical fingerprints within the same non-graph-cell-line-side pipeline, illustrating that the transition to graph representations began on the drug side before it was extended, as described in Section 3, to the biological (cell-line, pathway) side of the prediction problem as well.

A distinct and influential thread reframed DRP as a generative modeling problem, motivated by the observation that raw sensitivity labels are noisy, sparse, and confounded by both technical (assay) and biological variance, and that generative models could denoise or impute these labels using structure in the transcriptomic response itself. Rampásek, Hidru, Smirnov, Haibe-Kains, and Goldenberg's (2019) Dr.VAE is representative: rather than predicting viability directly from baseline expression, it jointly models drug response and drug-induced transcriptomic perturbation using a variational autoencoder, outperforming standard classifiers for 23 of 26 tested FDA-approved drugs and demonstrating that explicitly modeling perturbation effects — not just baseline state — improves prediction. Jia et al.'s (2021) VAEN pursues a related generative strategy for a different purpose: rather than modeling perturbation, it uses a deep variational autoencoder to compress thousands of baseline gene expression features into a low-dimensional latent space, which is then coupled with an elastic-net regression model to impute drug response, and shows that this compressed representation transfers more robustly across cross-panel validation (e.g., from cell line panels to patient-derived data) than standard signature-gene approaches. Wei, Dong, and Wang's (2022) scPreGAN extends generative modeling to single-cell resolution, using a GAN-based encoder-dual-generator architecture to predict how individual cell types respond to a perturbation from unperturbed single-cell expression data — addressing the practical problem that perturbed single-cell data is far more expensive to collect than unperturbed baseline data, and thus that most cell types/perturbation combinations of interest are never directly measured.

These generative approaches share two properties relevant to the rest of this review. First, they treat interpretability as a secondary concern at best: VAE latent spaces are not designed to be human-interpretable, and none of Dr.VAE, VAEN, or scPreGAN report explanation-quality evaluations, in contrast to the explicitly interpretability-oriented methods discussed in Section 4. Second, and more consequentially for the field's trajectory, none of them exploit relational structure among genes, pathways, or molecular substructures — cell lines and drugs are encoded as unstructured vectors, discarding biologically meaningful topology (e.g., protein-protein interaction networks, metabolic pathway membership, molecular bond graphs) that GNN-based methods were subsequently designed to exploit (Liu et al., 2022; Peng et al., 2022).

### 2.2 Drug Synergy and Combination Prediction: Combinatorial Modeling Challenges

Drug synergy prediction inherits all the modeling challenges of single-drug DRP and compounds them with the combinatorial explosion of drug-pair (and, for higher-order regimens, drug-tuple) space, which makes exhaustive experimental screening infeasible and elevates the practical stakes of accurate computational prioritization (Pan et al., 2023). Pan, Ren, Lan, Li, and Huang's (2023) review frames the task around three modeling choices that recur across the DSP literature: how drug pairs are represented (independently encoded and later fused, versus jointly encoded as an interaction graph), how synergy is scored (Loewe additivity, Bliss independence, ZIP, and related reference models), and how cell-line context is incorporated. Abbasi and Rousu's (2024) mini-review quantifies the field's recent growth, noting more than thirty original machine learning DSP methods published since 2021 alone, the clear majority deep-learning-based, and identifies a persistent and important limitation: even the best-performing methods solve the "known drug, known cell line" prediction scenario well, but degrade substantially on scenarios involving unseen drugs or unseen cell lines — a generalization gap that directly parallels the cross-dataset generalization problem documented for single-drug DRP by Partin et al. (2026, discussed in Section 2.1 and revisited in Section 6).

Two later 2024–2025 reviews extend this synthesis specifically toward deep learning. Wang, Wang, and Liu (2024) survey deep-learning DSP architectures and their evaluation protocols, cataloguing the field's rapid diversification into autoencoder, attention-based, and — increasingly — graph-based designs, while flagging inconsistent benchmark splitting practices (random versus leave-drug-out versus leave-cell-line-out) as a threat to comparability across studies. The two candidate reviews resolving the seed-list title ambiguity noted in Section 1.3 — Torkamannia, Omidi, and Ferdousi (2022) and Li, Zhang, Zheng, and Su (2025) — are complementary rather than redundant despite their similar titles: the earlier paper surveys the pre-2022 machine-learning DSP landscape broadly (feature-based, network-based, and early deep learning methods), while the 2025 review is narrower and later, focused specifically on deep learning DSP methods and incorporating post-2022 graph-based and transformer-based architectures, giving readers of this review a before/after view of the field's graph-based turn from within the DSP-review literature itself.

The choice of synergy reference model is itself a recurring source of methodological inconsistency documented across the DSP-review literature. Loewe additivity, Bliss independence, the highest single agent (HSA) model, and the more recent ZIP (zero interaction potency) model each define "expected" non-interactive combined effect differently, and because synergy scores are computed as a deviation from this expectation, a drug pair can be labeled synergistic under one reference model and merely additive under another (Pan et al., 2023). Pan et al. (2023) note that most DSP method papers select a single scoring convention (often ZIP or Loewe, following the convention of whichever public screening dataset — e.g., DrugComb or the O'Neil dataset — they train on) without systematically testing sensitivity to this choice, meaning that reported synergy-prediction accuracy is implicitly conditioned on a scoring convention that is rarely treated as a variable of interest in its own right. This adds a layer of comparability difficulty on top of the train/test-splitting inconsistency flagged by Wang, Wang, and Liu (2024), compounding the evaluation-protocol concerns that Section 5.2 returns to.

Architecturally, the DSP literature converges strongly on graph representations for the same reasons documented in Section 2.1 for DRP, but with an added relational dimension: drug pairs interact, so several DSP methods construct explicit drug-drug or drug-target-drug interaction graphs rather than encoding each drug independently, a design choice examined in depth in Section 3.2. Besharatifard and Vafaee (2024) provide the DSP-specific counterpart to the general GNN surveys discussed in Section 3, systematically comparing GNN model families applied to synergy prediction and concluding — consistent with Abbasi and Rousu (2024) — that graph-based methods now outperform non-graph deep learning baselines on standard benchmarks, but that this advantage narrows or disappears under the harder unseen-drug/unseen-cell-line generalization regime.

### 2.3 Summary of Theme 1

The pre-graph and non-graph DRP/DSP literature establishes three durable findings that structure the rest of this review. First, no single input representation dominates across settings (Xiao et al., 2025; Okyere et al., 2025), which motivates the field's continued architectural experimentation rather than convergence on one method. Second, generative modeling of perturbation and latent structure (Rampásek et al., 2019; Jia et al., 2021; Wei et al., 2022) improved robustness and label quality but did not, by itself, confer interpretability or exploit relational biological structure. Third, and most consequentially, the generalization gap to unseen drugs and cell lines — documented independently for DRP (Partin et al., 2026) and DSP (Abbasi & Rousu, 2024; Besharatifard & Vafaee, 2024) — is a cross-cutting weakness that predates and persists through the graph-based turn, and is revisited as a central research gap in Section 6.

## 3. Graph Neural Network Architectures for Drug Response and Synergy Prediction

### 3.1 GNN Foundations and Their Adoption in Cancer Drug Response Prediction

The GNN architectures used throughout the DRP/DSP literature build on a small set of foundational designs developed for general graph-structured data. Kipf and Welling's (2017) Graph Convolutional Network (GCN) introduced an efficient, spectrally motivated first-order approximation that aggregates each node's features with those of its neighbors via a shared, learned transformation, scaling linearly in the number of edges — a design that became the default "layer type" for graph representation learning. Veličković et al.'s (2018) Graph Attention Network (GAT) relaxed the GCN's fixed, degree-normalized neighbor-aggregation weights in favor of learned, masked self-attention coefficients over each node's neighborhood, allowing the model to differentially weight the influence of different neighbors — a property that, as discussed in Section 4, later proved doubly useful because attention weights are also directly interpretable as (weak) importance signals.

A further architectural refinement, relational graph convolution (RGCN) and its attention-augmented counterpart (RGAT), extends the base GCN/GAT operator to graphs with multiple distinct edge types by learning a separate transformation (RGCN) or attention mechanism (RGAT) per relation type, rather than treating all edges as interchangeable. This relational extension is architecturally important for DRP specifically because the biological graphs constructed from multi-omic data are rarely homogeneous: a cell-line/gene/pathway network typically contains structurally distinct edge types (e.g., protein-protein interaction, pathway co-membership, gene-drug target relationships) whose semantics differ, and collapsing them into a single undifferentiated edge type — as a plain GCN or GAT would require — discards exactly the kind of relational specificity that motivated the move to graph representations in the first place. As detailed in Section 4.2, this relational extension is precisely what allows XGDP (Wang et al., 2025) to evaluate GCN, GAT, RGCN, and RGAT as a graded architectural comparison, isolating how much of its predictive and explanatory performance is attributable to relation-awareness specifically rather than to graph structure in general.

Peng, Liu, Dai, Yu, and Wang's (2022) NIHGCN illustrates how these primitives were adapted to the specific bipartite structure of DRP: cell lines, drugs, and known response edges are jointly represented as a heterogeneous network, with a parallel graph convolution layer aggregating neighborhood-level features and a separate "neighborhood interaction" layer explicitly modeling element-wise feature interactions between a cell line and drug's respective neighborhoods, before predicting response as a linear correlation of the resulting representations. Liu et al.'s (2022) GraphCDR takes a complementary approach, constructing a GNN over multi-omic cell-line profiles and drug chemical structure jointly with known response edges, and augmenting the standard supervised objective with a contrastive learning regularizer; ablations show that the contrastive component, the multi-omic biological features, and the known-response graph structure are each independently necessary for GraphCDR's accuracy gains over non-graph baselines, providing direct evidence that relational structure — not just architectural novelty — drives the improvement.

Contrastive and multi-modal fusion strategies recur across several later GNN-based DRP methods, suggesting a maturing architectural consensus beyond the base GCN/GAT choice. Li, Guo, Gao, and Wang's (2023) MMCL-CDR aligns cancer cell-line representations across omic and cell-morphology-image modalities via contrastive learning before fusing them with graph-derived drug structure representations, reporting gains attributed specifically to the cross-modal alignment step. Duan et al. (2026) extend the fusion idea to a different modality pairing, combining molecular graph representations with textual (literature/knowledge-derived) drug descriptions in a dual-view contrastive framework (CLMT), reflecting a broader trend of treating drug representation as inherently multi-modal rather than graph-only. At the pathway level, Yoon and Lee's (2026) GCNPath departs from purely data-driven neighborhood learning by explicitly constraining the GNN's message-passing structure to follow curated biological pathway topology rather than a learned or correlation-derived graph, explicitly trading some representational flexibility for a built-in mechanistic prior — a design choice that anticipates, though does not itself constitute, the explainability-motivated architectures reviewed in Section 4. Two further method papers in the reviewed seed corpus, Liu and Zhang's (2023) SubCDR and Yu and Fan's (2025) DrugS, are notable for pursuing interpretability as a first-class design goal within a predictive (not necessarily GNN-only) architecture — SubCDR by decomposing drugs and cell lines into interacting "subcomponents" whose pairwise contributions are traceable to the final prediction, and DrugS by pairing prediction with downstream mechanistic case studies (e.g., identifying CDK, mTOR, and apoptosis inhibitors that reverse ibrutinib resistance) — situating them as a bridge to the explicitly XAI-labeled methods of Section 4 even though neither uses the formal GNNExplainer/attribution toolkit reviewed there. Finally, at the infrastructure level, Jiang and Li's (2024) DeepDR responds to this architectural proliferation not with a new model but with a unifying software library implementing 135 deep learning DRP model configurations across nine drug encoders and nine cell encoders, reflecting the field's recognition that architectural comparison had outpaced reproducible tooling.

### 3.2 GNN Architectures for Drug Synergy Prediction

GNN adoption in drug synergy prediction follows a similar trajectory but with an architectural feature specific to the pairwise nature of the task: several methods construct an explicit graph over the interaction between the two drugs' representations, rather than simply concatenating two independently computed drug embeddings. Wang, Wang, and Wei's (2023) AttenSyn exemplifies this pattern, first extracting per-drug latent features via a graph neural network over each drug's molecular graph, then applying an attention-based pooling module specifically designed to learn interactive information between the two drugs' feature sets, which the authors show both improves predictive accuracy relative to independent-encoding baselines and, as discussed in Section 4.2, doubles as a source of substructure-level interpretability. Besharatifard and Vafaee's (2024) survey situates AttenSyn within a broader family of GNN-based DSP architectures — including graph autoencoder, graph isomorphism network, and knowledge-graph-augmented variants — that share this same core design tension between representing each drug's internal molecular structure and representing the emergent interaction between two drugs' structures, concluding that architectures which explicitly model both levels tend to outperform those that model only one.

Structurally, this makes GNN-based DSP methods somewhat more architecturally elaborate than their single-drug DRP counterparts reviewed in Section 3.1, since they must represent (at minimum) two molecular graphs, an interaction between them, and typically a cell-line context vector, in a single forward pass. This added complexity is directly relevant to the explainability challenge taken up in Section 4: an explanation for a synergy prediction must, in principle, attribute the outcome not just to substructures within a single drug but to the joint interaction between substructures across both drugs and the cell-line context — a harder attribution target than in single-drug DRP, and one that, as shown in Section 4.2, only a subset of XAI-GNN methods (e.g., SANEpool) directly address.

This architectural elaboration also has a direct consequence for how DSP-GNN methods are evaluated: because the prediction target is a drug *pair* rather than a single drug, the space of possible train/test partitions is correspondingly larger — a new combination can be "unseen" because one of its two constituent drugs is unseen, because its cell-line context is unseen, or because the specific pairing itself is unseen even when both individual drugs were seen in other combinations during training. Besharatifard and Vafaee (2024) note that most DSP-GNN papers report results under only one or two of these partition types, typically the easiest (random split) and one harder variant, rather than the full matrix of possible generalization regimes, which compounds the train/test-splitting inconsistency and scoring-convention inconsistency already flagged in Section 2.2 and further limits the extent to which reported synergy-prediction accuracies can be compared across papers that use nominally the same benchmark dataset.

### 3.3 Summary of Theme 2

Three patterns emerge from the GNN-architecture literature. First, GCN and GAT (Kipf & Welling, 2017; Veličković et al., 2018) function as the shared architectural substrate beneath nearly every reviewed DRP and DSP method, with subsequent work innovating primarily in how graphs are constructed (heterogeneous cell-line/drug/response networks, pathway-constrained graphs, drug-drug interaction graphs) and how representations are fused (contrastive multi-omic/multi-modal alignment) rather than in the base message-passing operator itself. Second, contrastive learning has emerged as a near-default regularization strategy across both DRP (GraphCDR, MMCL-CDR, CLMT) and, implicitly, DSP methods, consistently reported as independently contributing to accuracy gains in ablation studies. Third, a small but important subset of methods (SubCDR, DrugS, GCNPath) build interpretability-adjacent design choices — subcomponent decomposition, pathway-constrained topology, mechanistic case studies — directly into the architecture without adopting the formal post-hoc XAI toolkit reviewed next, suggesting two parallel and only partially converging traditions for achieving interpretable DRP/DSP: architecturally-constrained "white-box-by-design" models versus post-hoc explanation of otherwise black-box GNNs.

## 4. Explainable AI for Graph Neural Networks in Drug Discovery and Mechanism Prediction

### 4.1 XAI Methods and Taxonomies for GNNs

The explainability methods applied to GNNs in the DRP/DSP literature are, almost without exception, adaptations of a small set of techniques developed for graph machine learning generally rather than bespoke inventions for this domain. Ying, Bourgeois, You, Zitnik, and Leskovec's (2019) GNNExplainer is the foundational and most widely reused method: framed as a model-agnostic, post-hoc optimization procedure, it identifies a compact subgraph and a small subset of node features that maximize mutual information with a given GNN's prediction, and it is explicitly the method used by XGDP (Section 4.2) to recover mechanistically interpretable substructures. Sundararajan, Taly, and Yan's (2017) Integrated Gradients offers a complementary, gradient-based attribution approach, satisfying sensitivity and implementation-invariance axioms by integrating gradients along a path from a neutral baseline to the actual input; unlike GNNExplainer, it requires no auxiliary optimization and is directly differentiable, which XGDP exploits by combining it with GNNExplainer for cross-validated attribution rather than relying on either method alone. Attention weights (Veličković et al., 2018) constitute a third, architecturally "free" interpretability signal: because attention-based GNN layers already compute per-neighbor importance weights as part of the forward pass, several DRP/DSP methods (e.g., AttenSyn, SANEpool) repurpose these weights directly as explanations rather than applying a separate post-hoc method, though this practice is contested in the general XAI-GNN literature, as discussed below.

Two recent surveys map this method space comprehensively. Kakkad, Jannu, Sharma, Aggarwal, and Medya's (2023) survey organizes GNN explainability methods along axes of instance-level versus model-level explanation, self-interpretable versus post-hoc explanation, and factual versus counterfactual explanation, and — most relevantly for this review — explicitly cautions that attention weights, despite their popularity as an interpretability proxy, do not reliably satisfy the faithfulness properties (i.e., correspondence between the explanation and the model's actual decision process) that formal post-hoc methods like GNNExplainer are designed to guarantee, a caution echoed by Rao, Zheng, and Yang's (2022) quantitative benchmarking study. Nandan, Mitra, and De's (2025) GraphXAI survey extends this taxonomy with an applied lens spanning fraud detection, drug development, and network analysis, classifying methods into post-hoc and self-interpretable categories and explicitly identifying drug discovery as one of the highest-stakes application domains for rigorous (as opposed to attention-weight-only) explanation. Rao, Zheng, and Yang (2022) provide the methodologically most rigorous contribution to this sub-literature: rather than proposing a new explainer, they construct five XAI-specific molecular property benchmarks with ground-truth substructure annotations and quantitatively evaluate six XAI methods across four GNN architectures against medicinal-chemist judgments, finding substantial variation in fidelity and stability across methods and — critically for this review's central topic — no single explainer dominating across all model/task combinations, a finding with direct implications for how confidently mechanistic claims from any single XAI-GNN DRP/DSP paper should be interpreted (see Section 6).

Beyond the GNN-specific toolkit of subgraph optimization, gradient attribution, and attention, the broader drug-discovery XAI literature also encompasses model-agnostic feature-attribution methods — SHAP (Shapley additive explanations) and LIME (local interpretable model-agnostic explanations) — and counterfactual and surrogate-model approaches, all catalogued by Gangwal and Lavecchia (2026). These methods are architecture-agnostic by design: SHAP and LIME approximate a black-box model's local behavior around a given prediction using cooperative-game-theoretic or locally-fit-surrogate reasoning respectively, rather than depending on differentiability or graph structure, which in principle makes them applicable to any DRP/DSP model regardless of whether its backbone is a GNN, a variational autoencoder, or a gradient-boosted tree. In practice, however, none of the GNN-specific DRP/DSP methods reviewed in Section 4.2 report SHAP or LIME baselines alongside their GNNExplainer/Integrated-Gradients/attention-based explanations, despite Gangwal and Lavecchia (2026) and Rao et al. (2022) both treating SHAP as a standard comparator in the general molecular-XAI literature — an omission that leaves open whether GNN-native explainers actually outperform architecture-agnostic alternatives on DRP/DSP tasks specifically, or whether their adoption instead reflects convenience given that the underlying models are already GNNs.

Two broader surveys situate this XAI-GNN toolkit within drug discovery specifically, rather than graph learning generally. Gangwal and Lavecchia's (2026) survey proposes a multidimensional taxonomy of XAI approaches for drug discovery organized by input modality, model transparency, and interpretability objective, critically comparing SHAP, LIME, saliency maps, attention, surrogate models, counterfactuals, and causal inference alongside evaluation metrics of fidelity, stability, sparsity, and interpretability — offering the most direct bridge in the reviewed literature between generic XAI methodology and drug-discovery-specific evaluation needs. Lavecchia's (2025) companion review frames the underlying motivation explicitly: predictive power alone is pharmacologically insufficient, and explainable methods must additionally support mechanistic insight — target identification, pathway attribution, off-target risk assessment — that a pure accuracy metric cannot certify. Berry and Cheng (2025) and Zhang et al. (2025) provide, respectively, a focused survey of GNNs for drug discovery and a comprehensive review spanning molecular property prediction, virtual screening, generation, and knowledge-graph applications; both treat explainability as one dimension of a larger GNN-for-drug-discovery landscape rather than as their central focus, and both, notably, cite the relative scarcity of explainability work specifically within cancer drug response/synergy prediction (as opposed to general molecular property prediction) as an open area — directly corroborating this review's motivating premise.

### 4.2 XAI-GNN Applications in Drug Response, Synergy, and Mechanism Prediction

The paper of central interest to this review, Wang, Kumar, and Rajapakse's (2025) XGDP ("Drug discovery and mechanism prediction with explainable graph neural networks"), sits at the direct intersection of Sections 3.1 and 4.1: it evaluates four GNN backbones — GCN, GAT, and their relational extensions RGCN and RGAT, the latter incorporating edge-type-specific message passing to capture heterogeneous biological interaction types — on cancer cell-line drug response prediction using GDSC and CCLE data, and, rather than treating explainability as an afterthought, applies both GNNExplainer and Integrated Gradients to every trained model to extract convergent, cross-validated mechanistic explanations. XGDP's central methodological contribution is this dual-explainer design: because GNNExplainer (subgraph-optimization-based) and Integrated Gradients (gradient-attribution-based) rest on different mathematical foundations, agreement between the two provides stronger evidence of a genuine, non-artifactual mechanistic signal than either method alone — directly operationalizing the faithfulness concern raised abstractly by Kakkad et al. (2023) and empirically documented by Rao et al. (2022). The paper's headline case study — recovering an EGFR-pathway-linked explanation for afatinib sensitivity, consistent with afatinib's known EGFR-inhibitor mechanism of action — functions as a form of external validation against established pharmacology, a validation strategy notably rarer in the broader XAI-GNN-for-DRP literature than one would expect given its evidentiary importance (see Section 6). XGDP thus exemplifies what this review identifies as the field's most advanced but still uncommon pattern: architecture-agnostic, multi-method, externally-validated mechanistic explanation, applied at the scale of a full DRP benchmark rather than a handful of illustrative examples.

Several earlier and contemporaneous methods approach the same integration from different architectural starting points, each with distinct strengths and limitations relative to XGDP. Wang, Zhou, Zhang, Mo, and Wang's (2023) XMR pursues interpretability primarily through architectural constraint rather than post-hoc attribution: it combines a "visible neural network" whose hidden-layer structure is explicitly constrained to mirror five curated Reactome pathways (cell cycle, DNA repair, disease, signal transduction, metabolism) with a separate GNN branch for drug structure, so that genomic-side interpretability is guaranteed by architecture rather than recovered after training — a design philosophy that trades some of GNNExplainer's post-hoc flexibility (applicable to any pretrained GNN) for interpretability that requires no separate explanation step but is limited to the fixed pathway ontology built into the network. Dong, Zhang, Chen, Payne, and Li's (2023) SANEpool addresses the drug-synergy-specific attribution challenge identified in Section 3.2 — attributing a joint two-drug outcome rather than a single-drug outcome — via a self-attention-based hierarchical graph pooling layer that computes attention scores over genes and gene-gene connections based on both genomic features and network topology, explicitly designed to surface core sub-networks of synergy-relevant genes and thereby reveal underlying therapeutic targets and combination mechanisms, directly extending single-drug substructure attribution to the combinatorial synergy setting. Shi et al.'s (2025) DRExplainer targets a related but distinct interpretability property that neither XGDP nor XMR emphasizes: rather than (or in addition to) qualitative case-study validation, it introduces a quantifiable interpretability metric grounded in a curated biological ground-truth benchmark, operating over a directed bipartite drug-response graph via a directed graph convolutional network, and reporting that its explanations outperform another graph-based explanation baseline on this quantitative measure — a direct, if still narrow, response to Rao et al.'s (2022) call for benchmarked rather than purely illustrative XAI-GNN evaluation. Wang, Wang, and Wei's (2023) AttenSyn, introduced architecturally in Section 3.2, illustrates the attention-as-explanation pattern flagged as methodologically contested in Section 4.1: its attention-based pooling module is presented as directly visualizable and interpretable, surfacing "crucial substructures" in each drug, but — consistent with Kakkad et al.'s (2023) caution — the paper does not independently validate these attention-derived substructures against a faithfulness metric or ground-truth benchmark in the way DRExplainer or XGDP's dual-explainer design does, making its interpretability claims comparatively weaker evidentially even though its predictive performance is competitive.

Read together, these five XAI-GNN DRP/DSP methods — XGDP, XMR, SANEpool, DRExplainer, and AttenSyn — span the full space mapped abstractly by the surveys in Section 4.1: XGDP and DRExplainer represent post-hoc, externally- or quantitatively-validated explanation; XMR represents self-interpretable, architecturally-constrained explanation; SANEpool represents attention-based explanation purpose-built for the combinatorial synergy setting; and AttenSyn represents the more common but evidentially weaker pattern of treating raw attention weights as sufficient explanation without independent validation. This span directly mirrors, at domain-specific scale, the general post-hoc/self-interpretable and validated/unvalidated distinctions that Kakkad et al. (2023), Nandan et al. (2025), and Rao et al. (2022) draw for GNN explainability broadly — evidence that the DRP/DSP XAI-GNN subfield, while still small, is not methodologically idiosyncratic but rather a direct, if lagging, instantiation of patterns already visible in the wider graph-explainability literature.

Beyond the headline afatinib/EGFR case study, XGDP's evaluation design is itself methodologically instructive for how DRP explainability should be reported. Because the model is trained and explained on the same GDSC/CCLE cell-line panels that underlie most of the DRP methods reviewed in Section 3.1, the resulting GNNExplainer/Integrated-Gradients subgraphs can be cross-referenced against curated drug-target and pathway databases external to the training data itself, rather than merely inspected for face-validity by the authors — the same principle DRExplainer (Shi et al., 2025) operationalizes quantitatively and that AttenSyn (Wang et al., 2023) does not apply to its attention-derived substructures at all. The comparison across GCN, GAT, RGCN, and RGAT backbones (introduced architecturally in Section 3.1) further allows XGDP to report not just which architecture predicts best, but whether the two explainers agree more or less depending on architecture — a diagnostic that is only possible because the paper treats explanation quality, not just predictive accuracy, as an evaluated outcome across every architectural variant rather than a property demonstrated once on a single chosen backbone. This architecture-by-explainer factorial evaluation design is, within the corpus reviewed here, unique to XGDP, and represents a template the field's future explanation-quality benchmarks (proposed in Section 6.2) could generalize.

### 4.3 Summary of Theme 3

The XAI-GNN literature for drug response and synergy prediction is best characterized as an early-stage but rapidly consolidating subfield. Methodologically, it draws almost entirely on three imported techniques — GNNExplainer-style subgraph optimization (Ying et al., 2019), gradient-based attribution via Integrated Gradients (Sundararajan et al., 2017), and repurposed attention weights (Veličković et al., 2018) — rather than developing bespoke domain-specific explainers, a pattern consistent with the field's overall youth. Evidentially, the reviewed methods split unevenly between rigorous, validated interpretability (XGDP's dual-method cross-validation and pharmacological case study; DRExplainer's quantitative ground-truth benchmark) and weaker, attention-only interpretability claims (AttenSyn) that the broader XAI-GNN survey literature (Kakkad et al., 2023; Rao et al., 2022) explicitly cautions against treating as faithful without further validation. Architecturally, self-interpretable, constraint-based designs (XMR, and, from Section 3.1, SubCDR and GCNPath) constitute a genuinely distinct tradition from post-hoc explanation of otherwise unconstrained GNNs (XGDP, DRExplainer), and the reviewed literature does not yet contain a systematic head-to-head comparison of these two traditions on shared DRP/DSP benchmarks — a gap taken up in Section 6.

## 5. Cross-Cutting Synthesis

### 5.1 Convergent Findings

Across all three themes, four findings recur with enough independence of source and method to be treated as robust. First, graph-structured representation of both drugs (molecular graphs) and biological context (multi-omic, pathway, or cell-line similarity networks) consistently outperforms unstructured feature-vector baselines, a finding independently supported by architecture papers (GraphCDR, NIHGCN, AttenSyn), by DSP-specific surveys (Besharatifard & Vafaee, 2024), and by DRP-specific surveys (Okyere et al., 2025) — even though, as Xiao et al. (2025) show, no single graph construction or representation choice dominates universally. Second, auxiliary contrastive or multi-modal alignment objectives (GraphCDR, MMCL-CDR, CLMT) reliably improve accuracy in ablation studies, suggesting that representation-learning regularization, not just base architecture choice, is now a first-order design lever in this literature. Third, and most directly relevant to this review's central topic, faithfulness — whether an explanation genuinely reflects a model's decision process rather than merely correlating with it — is treated as a serious, unresolved concern by every source that engages with XAI methodology rigorously (Kakkad et al., 2023; Rao et al., 2022; Gangwal & Lavecchia, 2026), and the strongest domain-specific responses to this concern (XGDP's dual-method agreement check, DRExplainer's quantitative benchmark) remain the exception rather than the norm among DRP/DSP-specific XAI-GNN papers. Fourth, shared public pharmacogenomic resources (GDSC, CCLE, DrugComb, and related panels, introduced in Section 1.1) function as the common evaluation substrate across essentially every method reviewed here, regardless of whether the paper's focus is prediction (Section 3) or explanation (Section 4) — a shared-infrastructure pattern that has enabled the field's rapid comparative progress but has also, as discussed next, concentrated nearly all reported gains within the biases of a small number of overlapping cell-line panels.

The relational-extension pattern documented in Section 3.1 — RGCN and RGAT's edge-type-specific message passing — also recurs as a convergent design response to a shared structural observation: biological graphs used in DRP/DSP are heterogeneous by nature, so that both predictive architecture papers (NIHGCN's parallel heterogeneous convolution) and the explainability-oriented literature (XGDP's relation-aware backbones) independently arrive at relation-awareness as a necessary, not merely beneficial, design choice once multi-omic or multi-edge-type data is involved.

### 5.2 Divergent Findings and Debates

The clearest unresolved debate concerns whether attention weights constitute valid explanations at all. Architecture papers that use attention mechanisms (AttenSyn, and, implicitly, GAT-based DRP models) frequently present attention weights as directly interpretable without further justification, while the dedicated XAI-GNN survey literature (Kakkad et al., 2023) and quantitative benchmarking work (Rao et al., 2022) explicitly documents cases where attention weights fail to correspond to ground-truth-relevant substructures — meaning a meaningful fraction of "interpretable" DRP/DSP claims in the applied literature may not withstand the faithfulness standards the XAI-methodology literature itself has established. A second, related divergence concerns architecturally-constrained ("self-interpretable") versus post-hoc explanation as competing philosophies: XMR and GCNPath argue implicitly, through their design, that guaranteed interpretability via biologically constrained architecture is preferable to unconstrained models paired with post-hoc explainers, while XGDP and DRExplainer implicitly argue the opposite — that post-hoc explanation of a maximally flexible (unconstrained) model preserves predictive performance while still enabling rigorous, validated interpretability. No source in this review directly compares these two philosophies on identical data and tasks, so this remains an open empirical question rather than a settled debate. Third, the DSP-review literature shows mild disagreement on evaluation protocol: Wang, Wang, and Liu (2024) and Abbasi and Rousu (2024) both flag inconsistent train/test splitting (random versus leave-drug-out versus leave-cell-line-out) as a comparability threat, but individual method papers in this review's corpus vary in which protocol they report, making some published performance comparisons across papers not strictly apples-to-apples.

### 5.3 Methodological Observations

Graph convolutional and graph attention layers (Kipf & Welling, 2017; Veličković et al., 2018), together with their relational/heterogeneous extensions (RGCN, RGAT, as used in XGDP), dominate the architectural landscape reviewed here, with contrastive learning as the most common auxiliary regularization strategy. By contrast, counterfactual explanation methods, causal-inference-based explanation, and surrogate-model explanation — all identified as legitimate XAI categories by the general surveys (Kakkad et al., 2023; Gangwal & Lavecchia, 2026) — are essentially absent from the DRP/DSP-specific method papers reviewed here, which cluster almost entirely around GNNExplainer-style subgraph attribution, gradient-based attribution, and attention. This concentration on a narrow slice of the available XAI toolkit, despite the wider toolkit's demonstrated relevance to drug discovery generally (Gangwal & Lavecchia, 2026; Lavecchia, 2025), is itself a methodological gap rather than a settled preference, since no source in this review offers evidence that the underused methods are less suited to this domain — their absence appears to reflect adoption lag rather than a principled exclusion.

## 6. Research Gaps and Future Directions

### 6.1 Identified Gaps

Six specific gaps emerge from this synthesis. First, there is no standardized, DRP/DSP-specific explanation-quality benchmark analogous to Rao et al.'s (2022) molecular-property-prediction benchmark; DRExplainer's (Shi et al., 2025) ground-truth-based quantitative evaluation is the closest analogue within this review's corpus, but it evaluates a single method on a single dataset rather than providing a shared benchmark others can adopt, leaving most XAI-GNN DRP/DSP papers to validate interpretability through illustrative case studies (as in XGDP's afatinib/EGFR example) rather than systematic, comparable metrics. Second, cross-dataset and cross-domain generalization — documented as a weakness independently for non-graph DRP (Partin et al., 2026), DSP broadly (Abbasi & Rousu, 2024), and GNN-based DSP specifically (Besharatifard & Vafaee, 2024) — has not been jointly examined together with explanation faithfulness: it is unknown whether a model's mechanistic explanations remain stable and biologically sensible when the model itself is applied out-of-distribution, a question with direct clinical-translation consequences. Third, external, wet-lab, or literature-curated validation of XAI-GNN-derived mechanistic hypotheses remains rare; XGDP's EGFR/afatinib case study and DrugS's ibrutinib-resistance case study are notable exceptions rather than a standard reporting practice, and the field would benefit from treating such validation as a required rather than optional component of any paper claiming mechanistic insight. Fourth, the attention-as-explanation practice identified in Section 5.2 as methodologically contested continues largely unaddressed within applied DRP/DSP papers (e.g., AttenSyn), even though the tools to test faithfulness (comparison against GNNExplainer/Integrated Gradients, as in XGDP, or against curated ground truth, as in DRExplainer) already exist and have been demonstrated within this same literature. Fifth, self-interpretable/architecturally-constrained methods (XMR, GCNPath, SubCDR) and post-hoc-explained methods (XGDP, DRExplainer) have never been directly compared on shared data and tasks, leaving practitioners without evidence-based guidance on which philosophy to adopt for a new DRP/DSP application, and leaving open whether either approach can be combined (e.g., a pathway-constrained GNN additionally explained via GNNExplainer) to capture the benefits of both. Sixth, as noted in Section 4.1, architecture-agnostic explanation methods (SHAP, LIME) that are standard comparators in the general molecular-property XAI literature (Gangwal & Lavecchia, 2026; Rao et al., 2022) are essentially untested against GNN-native explainers (GNNExplainer, Integrated Gradients, attention) within the DRP/DSP domain specifically, leaving it unclear whether the field's near-exclusive reliance on GNN-native methods reflects a genuine performance advantage or simply the convenience of applying an explainer from the same architectural family as the underlying model.

### 6.2 Proposed Research Agenda

Future work should prioritize, first, the construction of a shared DRP/DSP explanation-quality benchmark, following Rao et al.'s (2022) molecular-property-prediction template but grounded in curated cancer pathway/target databases, against which any new XAI-GNN DRP/DSP method could be quantitatively scored rather than validated only through anecdote. Second, studies should jointly evaluate predictive generalization and explanation stability under distribution shift — for instance, re-running XGDP-style dual-explainer analysis on the cross-dataset splits introduced by Partin et al. (2026) — to determine whether mechanistic explanations degrade gracefully or fail unpredictably outside the training distribution. Third, given the demonstrated value of external pharmacological validation in XGDP and DrugS, subsequent XAI-GNN DRP/DSP papers should treat at least one literature- or database-cross-checked mechanistic case study as a minimum reporting standard, analogous to how ablation studies became a de facto minimum standard for the architecture papers reviewed in Section 3. Fourth, direct comparative studies between self-interpretable/constrained architectures and post-hoc-explained unconstrained architectures — and hybrids combining both, such as a pathway-constrained backbone additionally probed with GNNExplainer — are needed to resolve the open architectural-philosophy debate identified in Section 5.2. Fifth, extending the underused XAI categories identified in Section 5.3 — particularly counterfactual explanation (e.g., "what minimal change to this molecule would eliminate the predicted synergy/sensitivity") — into the DRP/DSP domain would directly serve the drug-discovery use case of this review's central interest, since counterfactual explanations map naturally onto medicinal chemistry's own practice of iterative structural modification, a connection the current subgraph/attribution-dominated toolkit does not directly support. Sixth, future DRP/DSP-specific XAI papers should routinely report at least one architecture-agnostic baseline (SHAP or LIME) alongside their GNN-native explainer, following the practice already standard in the general molecular-XAI benchmarking literature (Rao et al., 2022; Gangwal & Lavecchia, 2026), so that claims of GNN-native explainer superiority in this domain rest on direct comparison rather than absence of an alternative.

## 7. Conclusion

### 7.1 Key Takeaways

This review establishes six key conclusions. First, graph neural networks have become the dominant architecture for both drug response and drug synergy prediction because they exploit relational structure — molecular and biological — that earlier feature-vector and generative approaches discarded, though no single graph construction or GNN variant is universally superior. Second, explainability methods applied to these GNNs are overwhelmingly imported rather than domain-specific, concentrated on GNNExplainer-style subgraph optimization, gradient-based attribution, and attention weights, with the last of these carrying an evidentially weaker and methodologically contested status than the other two, and with architecture-agnostic alternatives (SHAP, LIME) essentially untested against them in this specific domain. Third, the paper of central interest to this review, XGDP (Wang et al., 2025), represents the current frontier of rigor in this subfield by combining two independent, mathematically distinct explanation methods with an externally-validated pharmacological case study, a combination that remains the exception rather than the norm among comparable methods (XMR, SANEpool, DRExplainer, AttenSyn). Fourth, cross-dataset generalization is a pervasive weakness across the entire DRP/DSP literature, independent of whether explainability is a design goal, and has not yet been studied jointly with explanation faithfulness — a gap made structurally more likely by the field's heavy reliance on a small, overlapping set of public pharmacogenomic panels (GDSC, CCLE, DrugComb) as the near-universal evaluation substrate. Fifth, the field currently supports two largely unreconciled interpretability philosophies — architecturally-constrained self-interpretability and post-hoc explanation of unconstrained models — whose relative merits for this specific domain remain empirically untested. Sixth, inconsistent methodological conventions that predate and are independent of the graph-based and explainability turns — divergent synergy scoring models (Loewe, Bliss, ZIP), inconsistent train/test splitting protocols, and partial rather than exhaustive reporting of generalization regimes for drug pairs — continue to limit how confidently any single reported accuracy or interpretability result in this literature can be compared against another.

### 7.2 Implications for Practice and Policy

For researchers designing the next generation of interpretable DRP/DSP systems, this review's most actionable implication is that predictive architecture choice and explanation method choice should not be treated as independent decisions: architectures that already carry an attention mechanism invite the methodologically weakest form of interpretability claim unless independently validated, while architectures paired with post-hoc, multi-method, externally-checked explanation (as in XGDP) currently offer the strongest evidentiary standard available in this literature. For groups pursuing mechanism-of-action or biomarker discovery specifically — the use case motivating this review's focus — adopting XGDP's dual-explainer validation pattern, and reporting at least one pharmacologically cross-checked case study, represents the most direct, currently demonstrated path to producing mechanistic claims that a domain expert, not just a benchmark metric, would find credible. For the broader precision-oncology translational pipeline, the persistent and independently documented cross-dataset generalization gap (Partin et al., 2026; Abbasi & Rousu, 2024; Besharatifard & Vafaee, 2024) is a caution against premature clinical deployment of any DRP/DSP model — interpretable or not — without explicit external-cohort validation, since an explanation attached to a poorly generalizing prediction offers false reassurance rather than genuine mechanistic insight. Finally, for readers evaluating or reviewing work in this space — including thesis committees and journal reviewers assessing a new XAI-GNN DRP/DSP submission — this review's synthesis suggests a concrete, minimal evaluation checklist distilled directly from its strongest exemplars: report predictive accuracy under at least one hard generalization split (unseen drug or unseen cell line, following Partin et al., 2026), report explanation faithfulness via either a second, independent explanation method (as in XGDP's GNNExplainer/Integrated-Gradients agreement check) or a quantitative ground-truth benchmark (as in DRExplainer), and report at least one mechanistic case study cross-checked against an external pharmacological or pathway database rather than validated only by visual plausibility. No single method reviewed here satisfies all three criteria simultaneously, which is itself the clearest evidence that this remains an open, actionable frontier rather than a solved methodological problem.
